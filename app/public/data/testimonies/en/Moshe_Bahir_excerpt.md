Moshe Bahir, born Shkalek in Plock, Poland, who later lived in Ramat Gan and worked for Bank Hapoalim, was a fourteen-year-old boy when he, his mother, and his brother were selected at Komarow for deportation to Sobibor, after his father—identified by a card as an airport worker—was taken away from the market. On the journey and at the camp, his mother shared her last slice of bread with other children, an act that underscored the collapse of family protection into hunger and doom, and at the camp’s exit Oberscharführer Gustav Wagner halted Bahir and said “Du bist ein Mann,” sending him toward the male labor group while his mother and brother were sent to the gas chambers; Bahir never saw them again. His account, including the train’s scale of roughly 2,500 people and his first task of clearing huts for victims, illuminates the cruel arithmetic of Sobibor—the way “work” was weaponized to sustain murder—and preserves a piercing, human witness to the machinery of genocide that shaped the Holocaust’s history.
