Gedalia Ben-Zvi, a sixteen-year-old painter and teacher of painting from Bratislava, Slovakia, testified about his deportation at the war’s onset and transportation on 25 March 1942 first to Majdanek and then to Auschwitz in a mission framed as supplying a dextrin factory, among twenty-five doctors and about fifty children who were taken as apprentices. He recounted the brutal journey in freight cars, his arrival at Birkenau, and the transformation of Auschwitz into a vast complex where Birkenau became the principal camp, housing the infamous crematoria with gas chambers located inside their buildings and a landscape of divisions known to prisoners as the Stammlager, Frauenlager, and various subcamps linked by the notorious road from the railway to the gas chambers. Ben-Zvi’s eyewitness account captures the scale, organization, and bureaucratic logic of the extermination system, providing a stark, essential record of how a vast, meticulously administered machinery of murder operated and how memory of these places anchors the historical understanding of the Holocaust.
