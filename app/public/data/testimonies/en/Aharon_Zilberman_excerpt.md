Aaron Silbermann of Ness Tsiona identified his brother-in-law Gershon Willner, a dentist who had moved to Lvov and held an Argentinian passport, seeking shelter with Silbermann’s family after the outbreak of the German-Russian war. He recounted how two Gestapo men, wearing black uniforms with the skull insignia, entered their home with a Polish policeman and abducted Willner and his wife Miriam, after which they were moved from the Sanok jail to Rzeszów and then Tarnów, and were never heard from again. The only notice arrived on 25 June 1942—a telegram from the Sanok Gestapo claiming Willner had died of a heart attack in Auschwitz and demanding a permit and payment to retrieve the ashes, a chilling symptom of the bureaucratic brutality that left families paralyzed by fear.
