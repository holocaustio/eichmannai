Abba Kovner, a poet and commander of the Vilna Jewish underground that united movements from Betar to the Communists, delivered one of the trial's most powerful testimonies. He described entering Vilna with Red Army shock troops on July 13, 1944, finding a "desert of walls, empty streets," and encountering a mother whose child, after hiding for eleven months, whispered "Mother, are we allowed to cry already?" Kovner read the first manifesto of Jewish armed resistance, which he wrote on January 1, 1942: "Let us not go like sheep to the slaughter...All roads of the ghetto lead to Ponar, and Ponar means death." He described learning Eichmann's name from Anton Schmid, a Wehrmacht sergeant who helped Jews escape to Bialystok; when Kovner asked who controlled the extermination, Schmid replied: "Diese alle haben nichts zu sagen: es gibt einen Hund, der Eichmann heisst und der arrangiert alles" (These men have nothing to say: there is one dog called Eichmann, and he organizes everything). Kovner also recounted the agonizing death of Wittenberg, the underground's first commander, who surrendered to the Gestapo after the ghetto's own population, manipulated by the Nazis' collective punishment threats, turned against the fighters—an episode Kovner called "one of the greatest acts of heroism of Jewish underground fighting."
