Ya'akov Wiernik, a carpenter and construction worker from Rishon leZion, was brought to Treblinka on 23 August 1942 and remained there until 2 August 1943, where he built the barracks, the guard room, and the entrance gate with his companions. In his testimony he described a camp divided into Treblinka 1 and Treblinka 2, where women were led to a separate barrack and stripped of their clothes while men stood outside counting money and valuables; the women were then sent to gas chambers in a building with three chambers, and a larger adjacent complex housed ten chambers, with the operation lasting about forty to forty-five minutes, preceded by the Lazarette where the elderly were shot and followed by pits for graves and later burning on iron rails. After the war he drew a map of Treblinka and, while in Israel, built a life-sized model of the camp that was photographed and preserved at Kibbutz Lohamei Ha-Getta'ot; his testimony offered crucial, meticulously documented evidence of the camp’s architecture and the industrial scale of murder, shaping the Eichmann Trial and the broader memory of the Holocaust.
