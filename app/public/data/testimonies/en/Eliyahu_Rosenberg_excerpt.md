Eliahu Rosenberg, a thirty-five-year-old storeman who had worked at the Jaffa port and had lived in Warsaw until 1942, testified about his deportation there with his mother and three sisters to Treblinka in the summer of 1942. He recalled the ramp scene where a fellow Jew cried, “Moshe, grab a broom and save yourself,” and how, after a brutal selection by Kurt Franz, he narrowly survived by stepping forward for so‑called light work, only to witness the camp’s transformation into a killing site— the camouflaged gate, the pile of corpses, the sorting of parcels, and the transfer of bodies to deep graves and later their burning. He also learned that his mother and sisters had been buried in a grave at Treblinka, a personal, devastating detail that underscored the scale and intimate reach of the extermination system, and his testimony provides a granular, survivor’s account of the machinery of murder that the Eichmann Trial sought to document for history.
