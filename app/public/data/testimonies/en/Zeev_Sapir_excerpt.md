Ze'ev Sapir, a 20-year-old resident of the village of Dobradovo near Munkacs in Carpatho-Russia, testified about how his family and about 103 fellow villagers were confined with thousands of others in the Munkacs ghetto (then a Kalush brickyard) on 17 April 1944. He recalled that two weeks earlier Adolf Eichmann’s visit had been announced, and an order compelled older Jews—his own father among them—to perform welcome-work as the ghetto prepared for the SS commander; the visit was publicized in the local Nazi press, including Eichmann’s name and photograph, and the man walked at the head of a party of about thirty SS officers. Two days before the deportations began in May 1944, Sapir and his family, together with the entire community, were deported in a single railway wagon with more than a hundred people; his testimony is historically significant for tying Eichmann directly to the orchestration of Hungary’s deportations and for exposing the ordinary routines—the rolls, the propaganda, the forced labor—that facilitated mass murder.
