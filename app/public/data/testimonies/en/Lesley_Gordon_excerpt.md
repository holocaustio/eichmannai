Leslie Gordon, born in Budapest and raised among a Jewish family regarded as Polish citizens under Hungarian law, testified that in June 1941 he and his relatives—seven children and their parents—were interned by Hungarian authorities and handed over to German forces for deportation. He described a brutal journey: about three to four hundred people packed into eight train cars, later loaded onto German trucks marked with the Totenkopf, forced to disembark near Kolomea with the command Schweine-Juden, herunter!, and driven to walk eastward while those who hesitated or attempted to relieve themselves were shot on the spot by the SS. Only Gordon survived; his sister, who had become a Hungarian citizen by marriage, was spared and later joined him in Canada, while others perished along the road or in Buczacz, where he endured forced labor; his account starkly reveals the complicity of Hungarian authorities with Nazi deportations, the immense scale of family loss, and the human brutality that accompanied the early Holocaust across occupied Poland and into the Ukrainian borderlands.
