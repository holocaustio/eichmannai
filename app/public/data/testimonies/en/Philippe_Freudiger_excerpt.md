Pinchas Freudiger, also known as Philip von Freudiger, was a Budapest-born textile industrialist who ran the family factory and, after his father’s death in 1939, headed the Orthodox Jewish community while inheriting a noble title from his grandfather. He testified that the family enjoyed close relations with Hungary’s ruling ministries through long-standing service, yet witnessed how anti-Semitism intensified—culminating in the 1920 numerus clausus that restricted Jewish university admissions—and noted that territorial changes between 1938 and 1940 swelled Hungary’s population and its Jewish community from about 480,000 to roughly 800,000. Freudiger’s testimony illuminated the precarious arc of Hungarian Jewry, a story of prosperity and leadership shadowed by legal discrimination and political upheaval that set the stage for the catastrophe under examination at Nuremberg’s Eichmann trial.
