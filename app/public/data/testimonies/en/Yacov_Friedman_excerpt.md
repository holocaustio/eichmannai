Ya'akov Friedman, a clerk in the Tel Aviv municipality, testified that in late 1942, as a Polish Christian deemed Aryan, he was arrested in the Lublin district and transported to Majdanek, where he shared a hut with roughly three hundred Jewish inmates while a group of forty-seven Poles performed unproductive labor. He recalled the ritualized roll-calls, with the orders Muetze ab and Muetze auf enforced by blows, the brutal pace of assignments, a bread ration of one loaf for five men over two days, and the daily burden of hauling away the dead at dawn amid a regime of punishment and fear. He spoke of the camp’s mortality, dysentery, and the electrified fence through which suicides occurred, and he testified to witnessing hangings every day, while also learning from fellow inmates about transports of Jews—undressed, processed as if in disinfection, and then sent to gas-chamber–like showers—so that Majdanek stood as a stark exemplar of how routine brutality and mass murder were organized, recorded, and endured.
