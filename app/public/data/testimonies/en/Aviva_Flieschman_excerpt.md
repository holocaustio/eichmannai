Aviva Fleischmann, a Budapest hairdresser who was required to wear the yellow Star, testified that she heard a Gestapo man named Peter Hain speak of Adolf Eichmann and how “the Jews should be dealt with,” a name she would come to hear mentioned again in the months that followed. After Szálasi’s coup, Arrow Cross squads seized Jews from their homes, and Fleischmann, her blind father, her mother who served as his eyes, her sister, and other relatives were taken to the brick factory at Óbuda, passing by corpses with yellow badges along the road and through a city already emptied of its safety. There, thousands were packed into small brick cells, a stark portrait of how quickly leadership, persecution, and mass confinement interlocked in Budapest, linking the intimate spaces of daily life to the machinery of the Holocaust and underscoring Eichmann’s role in orchestrating deportation and murder.
