Margit Reich, a Budapest resident and wife of a man arrested in June 1944 and sent to the Kistarcsa camp, testified to the sudden rupture of her family life and to the precarious channels by which she learned of his fate. She recounted a postcard thrown from the freight car, bearing the note “Thrown out at the Karacsond station, it got wet but is nevertheless forwarded,” and a later letter written on toilet paper, in which her husband spoke of a long journey to Germany and urged their children to look after their mother, hoping for some miracle. Kept in her possession and authenticated by handwritten copies, these documents conveyed the intimate horror of deportation and, within the Eichmann trial, supplied a human counterpoint to the bureaucratic machinery of genocide, illustrating how one family clung to faith, memory, and small acts of documentation amid unimaginable peril.
