Kalman Teigmann, twenty years old and a mechanical fitter from the Warsaw Ghetto, testified about being torn from his Astra Werke factory job and, with his mother, loaded into overcrowded freight cars bound for Treblinka in September 1942. He described the claustrophobic voyage—the air barely entering through a small window, a stifling, chlorine-like stink, and Ukrainians rifling passengers of valuables while beating those who lagged or fainted, with several attempting to leap from the cars as shots rang out. Upon arrival, he recounted being driven off into the Treblinka yard through a gate, the platform scene and the signs from Polish railway workers that they were travelling to their deaths, and the layout of Camp 1 and Camp 2 that would reveal the mechanized horror of the extermination system—the kind of firsthand account that rendered the Eichmann trial a crucial record of the Holocaust.
