Adolf Rosenberg, a carpenter from Nove Mesto nad Vahom, Slovakia, testified that in 1941 his shop was Aryanized and he remained employed there as a technical adviser under a Ministry of the Interior permit while also being compelled to work at the Novaky camp, where about 100 men from his town were forced to construct the buildings that would supposedly belong to them in the future. He described the regime’s encroachment on daily life—the mandatory yellow badge, strict movement restrictions, and his living arrangement with his elderly parents in his own apartment, which the Hlinka Guard commander used as a storeroom—until his arrest in September 1944 and deportation to Sered, where German guards and local authorities controlled the detainees. The brutality he witnessed, including the beating of a Jewish prisoner named Grossmann by Knollmeyer, underscored how Slovak collaborators helped enforce Nazi policies, revealing a disturbing, integral facet of the Holocaust in occupied Slovakia and the coercive machinery that uprooted families and dismantled communities.
