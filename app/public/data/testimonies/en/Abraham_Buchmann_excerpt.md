Avraham Buchman, a carpenter from Givatayim and a former Polish army conscript who was captured in 1939, testified about his ordeal as a Jewish prisoner, moving through Stalag 3b near Jessnitz to Szubin, Lubliniec, Czestochowa, and eventually Lipowa 7 in Lublin. He recalled being counted at Lublin station and told that he was being handed a group of prisoners of war, with the officer warning that anyone who escaped would cost ten others their lives, and that the roughly six hundred prisoners remained in limbo, sharing a two-day stay at Lipowa 7 before a further march toward Lubartow, punctuated by the sound of distant gunfire from a barn. Buchman’s account illuminates the chilling, procedural nature of Nazi brutality—how Jews were segregated, depersonalized into numbers, and funneled through a chain of camps—providing a stark, human record of the early, mechanized violence that culminated in mass murder.
