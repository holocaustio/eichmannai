Henryk Ross, born in 1910, was a photographer who had worked for Polish newspapers before the war and, after the Lodz Ghetto was sealed in May 1940, served in the ghetto’s statistical department as a photographer until August 1944. He recalled a portrait of industrial-scale deprivation: a population of about 203,000 Jews at sealing, rotting rations and a daily bread allotment that proved inadequate, a soup of water with a scant handful of potatoes, cereals, and Kohlrüben, six to eight people crammed into single rooms, and bodies piling up in the wake of hunger and cold, with as many as 120 burials transported by the Burial Society in a single day. His testimony, grounded in meticulous records and photographs, offered stark, undeniable evidence of Nazi policies of starvation, overcrowding, and dehumanization within the Lodz Ghetto, making his account a pivotal contribution to the Eichmann Trial’s documentary record and to the historical memory of how ordinary life collapses under tyranny.
