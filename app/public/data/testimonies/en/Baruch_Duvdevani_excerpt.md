Baruch Duvdevani, an official in the Jewish Agency’s Immigration Department, described his postwar mission to locate Rabbi Kalunimus Shapiro’s writings and the long, painstaking search through Warsaw’s ruins that finally yielded a manuscript now known as The Holy Fire. He recounted that a milk jar preserved by Emanuel Ringelblum’s archival work was found in a ditch on Genzia Street, containing the rabbi’s sermons delivered in his synagogue and in the Schultz factory where he labored as a tailor, urging perseverance, study, and faith amid hunger and the destruction of religious life; the rabbi’s will directed that these writings be published in Israel. Duvdevani read passages that depicted the faltering yet indomitable hope for Torah and yeshivot, and he noted that Shapiro died in Trawniki, making the survival of his words all the more poignant as a witness to spiritual resistance and a source for the historical memory that the Eichmann Trial sought to illuminate.
