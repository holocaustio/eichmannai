Gustave M. Gilbert, then chairman of the Psychology Department at Long Island University, served as a U.S. Army military psychologist and intelligence officer stationed at Nuremberg to assess the defendants’ mental state and monitor the conduct of the trial. He administered intelligence and personality tests to all the defendants, maintained daily contact—from courtroom intermissions to their cells—and kept extensive notes—diary entries with verbatim quotations corroborated by documentary evidence—to ground psychological observations in record. Revealing his Jewish identity elicited little effect beyond occasional nerves from Streicher and Rosenberg, but his disciplined, professional testimony and the diary he kept furnished a rare, intimate portrait of the Nazi mind and the tribunal’s clinical pursuit of accountability.
