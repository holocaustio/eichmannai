Viteslav Diamant, born in 1901 in Czechoslovakia, was an electrician who worked in Theresienstadt, serving near the Council of Elders and wiring the workshops. He recalled the autumn of 1944 when Eichmann arrived and ordered the prisoners to assemble, a moment in which he, like others, was singled out and addressed with brutality as a “stinking Jew,” while he hesitated to leave his post and faced pressure and threats before returning to work. The incident—witnessed in the presence of Rahm and Sever and later attributed to Eichmann—laid bare the quotidian reach of top Nazi leadership into camp life, underscoring the systemic terror that Diamant and countless others endured; it also resonated with his earlier act of safeguarding secret documents that recorded orders and deportations, documents he hoped would survive to reveal the machinery of annihilation.
