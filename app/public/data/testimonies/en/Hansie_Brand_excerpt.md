Hansi Brand, born in Budapest, was a member of Budapest’s Relief and Rescue Committee (Va’ada) and, with her husband Joel Brand, ran a knitwear workshop while helping refugees who arrived from Germany, Austria, Poland, Slovakia, and even Yugoslavia; she looked after those who did not speak Hungarian, ensuring they were clothed, identified, housed, and provided with the necessary papers. After the German occupation on March 19, 1944, she recounted that the committee’s work went underground and shifted to obtaining Christian papers in large quantities to enable Jews to survive by passing as Christians, ordering the forms from printers and distributing them, while her husband coordinated couriers and organized the tiyulim, or escape excursions. Her testimony illuminates the clandestine rescue network within Hungary, revealing the courage and ingenuity of ordinary volunteers and the moral complexities of rescue, a crucial thread in the broader history of the Eichmann trial and the fight to preserve human life amid genocide.
